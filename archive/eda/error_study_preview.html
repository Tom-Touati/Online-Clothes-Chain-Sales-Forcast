  1. 42% of item totals land within ±20%, and the realistic item-level ceiling is about half. The median forecast is off by 24.5%, so the ±20% line cuts through the middle of the distribution; 59% are within ±30%. No cohort or simple rule reaches 60% in both halves of the backtest; the most confident tenth of items reaches 56%. Knowing the plans and one month of sales would lift the rate to 48%. Summed to look or product-type level the same forecasts are within ±20% 66% and 61% of the time.
  2. Absolute error is steady; the ratio to R3 is not. The system misses by 21–31% of units at every recent origin, while R3 misses by 41–131%. The 0.385 headline leans on three post-holiday origins where R3 is at its worst. Against a seasonal naive the system scores 0.62 on recent origins and 0.62 on all 13. The June 2025 origin, which forecast the same months as the deliverable, scored 0.69 × R3.
  3. The error is about which items, not how much in total. 42% of it sits in 5% of the forecasts. With every origin's total known in advance the score would move from 0.385 to 0.379. 61% of the error is under-forecast, and in 48% of it all five models are too low together.
  4. Re-slicing by cohort is exhausted. Across 34 cohort axes, no group bias survives a time-honest correction (best gain −0.0008). What remains is specific to items.
  5. The missing input is the roll-out plan. Launches that reached five or more additional own stores grew 4.9× R3 and were under-forecast by 37%; launches with flat distribution were over-forecast by 15%. Knowing distribution ahead is worth 0.014 of WAPE ÷ R3; a re-forecast after one month of sales is worth 0.048.
  6. The model remembers one season. Remove the one to three training origins that forecast the same calendar window a year earlier and the tree model's error rises by a median of +0.10 (once by +1.70). Removing as many other origins changes it by +0.01.
  7. The backtest is weaker evidence than its intervals suggest, for four reasons. First, each origin forecasts four months and origins are one month apart, so neighbouring origins are tested on mostly the same months: 13 origins behave like about 3 separate tests, and spacing them further apart does not change that (see Origins further apart). Second, 192 model variants were scored on those same origins and the winners kept; with that many tries, a gain under about 0.005 can be luck. Third, two of the adopted steps make the forecast worse on the four earliest origins. Fourth, the 100 targets were chosen knowing how they sold later, so the backtest sees them as items that out-perform: their bias of −15% comes from that choice and should not be corrected for.
  8. Three World Cup items are capped by hand. Three of the 100 targets are World Cup tops and shorts launched on 25 May. Sales will fall after the final on 19 July, and the models have never seen a World Cup, so they extend the June ramp: 53,270 units, with August above July for the main tee. The first submission capped them by hand; the workbooks built from the model chain afterwards did not carry the cap. It was restored on 17 November: 34,463 units. Is the total wrong, or the split between items?

The split. Suppose the forecast for all items together had been exactly right at every origin, with each item scaled to match: the rate would be 41%, no better than today. With the total of every product type exactly right it would be 44%. Only when the total of every product type within every look is known (about 250 small groups) does it reach 56%. So the misses come from which items sell within a group, not from the overall amount.

Can those group totals be estimated and fed in? Tested after a comment on this page: no. The groups are small (247 per origin, a median of 2 items, 41% of them a single item), so estimating a group total is nearly as hard as forecasting the item. A separate model of the group totals is worse than adding up the item forecasts (D240), and rescaling items to it lowers the hit rate from 41% to 36%. As inputs to a time-honest correction, the modelled group total and the group's forecast growth change nothing (0.389 against 0.388). The estimate would have to be accurate: rescaling to the true group total with ±10% noise gives 53%, ±15% gives 49%, ±20% gives 45% and ±30% gives 37%, below today. Our group totals are off by about ±32% weighted by units.

What about the group's recent sales? Also tested after a comment: no measurable gain. 21 inputs: for each item's type × look group, its look and its product type, the sales of the last three months, the trend against the three months before, the newest-month turn and the change on a year ago. The year-on-year change leans the right way (rank correlation with the miss +0.12, same sign at 10 of 13 origins). In a time-honest correction the block gives 0.386 against 0.388 without it, and 42.0% within ±20% against 42.1%. The difference (−0.0043 to +0.0001) includes zero, and the submitted system scores 0.385. Measured against all other groups instead (the group's share of all units, and its trend, newest-month turn and year-on-year change against the rest; 21 inputs) it is the same: 0.388, and 41.8% within ±20%.

And new stores opening? No gain either. 10 inputs: own stores trading at the origin, opened in the last three months and opening in the forecast window, each also weighted by the item's own-store share. They carry nothing at item level (rank correlation +0.01; 0.388 with them). Stores do drive volume: year on year, own-store sales grew 2.1× as the stores grew 2.2×. But an opening is the same fact for every item at an origin, so it cannot say which items gain. What does say it is the number of stores each item is itself added to during the window (rank correlation +0.28, seen afterwards). That is the allocation plan: the business holds it, the data does not.

How much tighter would item forecasts need to be? Take the spread of item errors around the forecast (their standard deviation in log terms, 0.35). With that spread and no bias, 43% of items land within ±20%, about what we see. Half of the items would land there if the spread shrank by 16%; 60% needs 32% and 70% needs 45%.

Counting noise is not the limit: a forecast equal to the true mean would land within ±20% 96% of the time, and 83% for the smallest fifth, where the system hits 28%.

Against R3 item by item

Asked in the session: WAPE ÷ R3 is a ratio of sums, so large items decide it. The item-equal version takes each item's own error relative to R3's, (|F − y| + 1) ÷ (|R3 − y| + 1), and then averages over items. How it is averaged matters. A plain average of the ratios is lopsided: an item twice as good counts 0.5 and an item twice as bad counts 2, and items where R3 happened to be almost exact give huge ratios. Even capped at 5× per item, the plain average puts the system at 0.886, close to R3, which does not match any other view. The geometric mean (GMRAE) fixes this, since a 2× better and a 2× worse item cancel. It is the standard item-equal choice, together with the median (MdRAE) and the share of items where the forecast is closer than R3.

Below 1 means better than R3; R3 itself scores 1 on every ratio column, and a forecast no better than R3 would be closer for about half the items. All 13 origins: the system's GMRAE is 0.482 against 0.516 for R3 + rules. The +1 unit keeps an exact R3 from dividing by zero; with +5 the system's GMRAE is 0.428.

The item-equal view agrees with the unit view on the ranking: the system beats R3 + rules and every single model on GMRAE and on the share closer than R3. It is less flattering: 0.413 against 0.385 in units, because the system gains most on large items. By volume fifth, the system's GMRAE runs from 0.659 on the smallest items to 0.249 on the largest. The 11-signal version (D307) helps in units (WAPE ÷ R3 0.3835 against 0.3851) but is level item by item (GMRAE 0.4122 against 0.4127): its gain sits on larger items.

Three things handle it.

  1. Re-forecast after the first month. In 65% of these forecasts the first month already ran well above pace. With the plans and one month of sales known, their error falls by 23% and 34% of them land within ±20%, against 20% without.
  2. The roll-out plan. This is the business's list of which stores each item will be sent to. 43% of these forecasts are items that reached two or more additional own stores during the window, against 22% of other forecasts. Their sales rose partly because they were on more shelves. The models could not know that on the forecast date; the business did. If the models are told what the business plans ahead (stores and partners added, discount depth, stock and sizes, new colours), the error on these forecasts is 10% smaller. The effect is modest because most of these misses are demand, not distribution.
  3. A watch list, for buying and for attention. The fifth of forecasts with the highest risk holds 40% of this error, and 39% of that fifth turn out all-too-low. Raising the whole fifth by the factor seen at earlier origins (1.06–1.18) brings the overall bias from −7% to −4% but leaves accuracy where it was (0.298 against 0.295 over the last 5 origins), because it also raises the flagged items that did not take off. So the flag should not move the forecast. It says which items to re-forecast first and where to buy above the forecast.

How the flag is identified. At each origin a classifier is trained on earlier forecasts whose results are known. Its target is yes or no: did all five models end below what sold. Its 23 inputs are all known on the forecast date. It gives each new forecast a risk between 0 and 1, and the flag is the fifth of forecasts with the highest risk at that origin. 39% of that fifth turn out all-too-low, against 13% of the lowest fifth.

Method

Definitions and limits

System. The submitted forecast (candidate_nx4, D350; the same units as candidate_nx3, with the D318 confidence rule and the D317 ranges): arithmetic mean of five members (rule agents, LightGBM, neural net, two TabPFN v2 variants) with the segment and breakout corrections (D273–D274), plus two hand rules on four of the 100 targets (out of stock, World Cup). The backtest scores the model system; the hand rules exist only in the 2026 workbook. Scores reproduce the log: 0.385 recent, 0.440 on all 13.

Scores. WAPE ÷ R3 = Σ|forecast − actual| ÷ Σ|R3 − actual| on 4-month item totals. Absolute error = Σ|forecast − actual| ÷ Σ actual. Bias = Σ forecast ÷ Σ actual − 1; negative is under-forecast.

Hindsight signals use what happened in the forecast window. They show what information is missing; they are not forecasts. "Stores" counts own stores with at least one sale, so part of the store signal is demand itself. Stock gaps are inferred from improbable runs of zero-sale days (D021), not from inventory.

Not covered. Month-level errors inside the window, and the buying simulation. The season-memory test uses the tree model alone. Files: eda/198c–198g, data/fk_err_master.parquet, data/fk_err_analysis.json; decisions D276–D277 in log.md.
