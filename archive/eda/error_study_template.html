  2. Absolute error is steady; the ratio to R3 is not. The system misses by {{abs_min}}–{{abs_max}} of units at every recent origin, while R3 misses by {{r3_min}}–{{r3_max}}.   3. The error is about which items, not how much in total. {{top5}} of it sits in 5% of the forecasts. With every origin's total known in advance the score would move from {{ratio_recent}} to {{tot_known}}. {{under_err}} of the error is under-forecast, and in {{all_under}} of it all five models are too low together.
  4. Re-slicing by cohort is exhausted. Across {{n_axes}} cohort axes, no group bias survives a time-honest correction (best gain {{best_th}}). What remains is specific to items.
  5. The missing input is the roll-out plan. Launches that reached five or more additional own stores grew {{yg_big_growth}}× R3 and were under-forecast by {{yg_big_bias}}; launches with flat distribution were over-forecast by {{yg_flat_bias}}. Knowing distribution ahead is worth {{voi_dist}} of WAPE ÷ R3; a re-forecast after one month of sales is worth {{voi_m1}}.
  6. The model remembers one season. Remove the one to three training origins that forecast the same calendar window a year earlier and the tree model's error rises by a median of {{ls_same_med}} (once by {{ls_same_max}}). Removing as many other origins changes it by {{ls_other_med}}.
  7. The backtest is weaker evidence than its intervals suggest, for four reasons. First, each origin forecasts four months and origins are one month apart, so neighbouring origins are tested on mostly the same months: 13 origins behave like about {{neff13}} separate tests, and spacing them further apart does not change that (see Origins further apart). Second, {{variants}} model variants were scored on those same origins and the winners kept; with that many tries, a gain under about {{null_max}} can be luck. Third, two of the adopted steps make the forecast worse on the four earliest origins. Fourth, the 100 targets were chosen knowing how they sold later, so the backtest sees them as items that out-perform: their bias of {{tgt_bias}} comes from that choice and should not be corrected for.
  8. Three World Cup items are capped by hand. Three of the 100 targets are World Cup tops and shorts launched on 25 May. Is the total wrong, or the split between items?

The split. Suppose the forecast for all items together had been exactly right at every origin, with each item scaled to match: the rate would be {{hit_origin_total}}, no better than today. With the total of every product type exactly right it would be {{hit_type}}. Only when the total of every product type within every look is known (about 250 small groups) does it reach {{hit_cell}}. So the misses come from which items sell within a group, not from the overall amount.

Can those group totals be estimated and fed in? Tested after a comment on this page: no. The groups are small ({{ce_per}} per origin, a median of {{ce_med}} items, {{ce_single}} of them a single item), so estimating a group total is nearly as hard as forecasting the item. A separate model of the group totals is worse than adding up the item forecasts (D240), and rescaling items to it lowers the hit rate from {{ce_then}} to {{ce_full}}. As inputs to a time-honest correction, the modelled group total and the group's forecast growth change nothing ({{ce_feat_w}} against {{ce_ref_w}}). The estimate would have to be accurate: rescaling to the true group total with ±10% noise gives {{ce_n10}}, ±15% gives {{ce_n15}}, ±20% gives {{ce_n20}} and ±30% gives {{ce_n30}}, below today. Our group totals are off by about ±{{ce_sd_w}} weighted by units.

What about the group's recent sales? Also tested after a comment: no measurable gain. {{gs_ng}} inputs: for each item's type × look group, its look and its product type, the sales of the last three months, the trend against the three months before, the newest-month turn and the change on a year ago. The year-on-year change leans the right way (rank correlation with the miss {{gs_rho_yoy}}, same sign at {{gs_same_yoy}} of 13 origins). In a time-honest correction the block gives {{gs_g_w}} against {{gs_ref_w}} without it, and {{gs_g_hit}} within ±20% against {{gs_ref_hit}}. The difference ({{gs_ci_lo}} to {{gs_ci_hi}}) includes zero, and the submitted system scores {{ratio_recent}}. Measured against all other groups instead (the group's share of all units, and its trend, newest-month turn and year-on-year change against the rest; {{gs_nr}} inputs) it is the same: {{gs_r_w}}, and {{gs_r_hit}} within ±20%.

And new stores opening? No gain either. {{gs_ns}} inputs: own stores trading at the origin, opened in the last three months and opening in the forecast window, each also weighted by the item's own-store share. They carry nothing at item level (rank correlation {{gs_rho_open}}; {{gs_s_w}} with them). Stores do drive volume: year on year, own-store sales grew {{gs_yoy_sales}}× as the stores grew {{gs_yoy_stores}}×. But an opening is the same fact for every item at an origin, so it cannot say which items gain. What does say it is the number of stores each item is itself added to during the window (rank correlation {{gs_rho_hind}}, seen afterwards). That is the allocation plan: the business holds it, the data does not.

How much tighter would item forecasts need to be? Take the spread of item errors around the forecast (their standard deviation in log terms, {{sigma}}). With that spread and no bias, {{normal_hit}} of items land within ±20%, about what we see. Half of the items would land there if the spread shrank by {{cut50}}; 60% needs {{cut60}} and 70% needs {{cut70}}.

Counting noise is not the limit: a forecast equal to the true mean would land within ±20% {{pois_all}} of the time, and {{pois_small}} for the smallest fifth, where the system hits {{hit_fifth1}}.

Against R3 item by item

Asked in the session: WAPE ÷ R3 is a ratio of sums, so large items decide it. The item-equal version takes each item's own error relative to R3's, (|F − y| + 1) ÷ (|R3 − y| + 1), and then averages over items. How it is averaged matters. A plain average of the ratios is lopsided: an item twice as good counts 0.5 and an item twice as bad counts 2, and items where R3 happened to be almost exact give huge ratios. Even capped at 5× per item, the plain average puts the system at {{ir_mean}}, close to R3, which does not match any other view. The geometric mean (GMRAE) fixes this, since a 2× better and a 2× worse item cancel. It is the standard item-equal choice, together with the median (MdRAE) and the share of items where the forecast is closer than R3.

Recent originsWAPE ÷ R3 (units)Geometric mean of item ratios (GMRAE)Median item ratio (MdRAE)Items closer than R3Plain mean of item ratios, capped at 5×

Below 1 means better than R3; R3 itself scores 1 on every ratio column, and a forecast no better than R3 would be closer for about half the items. All 13 origins: the system's GMRAE is {{ir_g13}} against {{ir_r13}} for R3 + rules. The +1 unit keeps an exact R3 from dividing by zero; with +5 the system's GMRAE is {{ir_g5}}.

The item-equal view agrees with the unit view on the ranking: the system beats R3 + rules and every single model on GMRAE and on the share closer than R3. It is less flattering: {{ir_g}} against {{ir_w}} in units, because the system gains most on large items. By volume fifth, the system's GMRAE runs from {{ir_f1}} on the smallest items to {{ir_f5}} on the largest. The 11-signal version (D307) helps in units (WAPE ÷ R3 {{ir_sig_w}}) but is level item by item (GMRAE {{ir_sig_g}}): its gain sits on larger items.

Asked in the session. R² is 1 when the forecast explains all the variation between items and 0 when it does no better than giving every item the same average. It is shown three ways, because the answer depends on what is to be explained.

In units, large items decide it. In logs every item counts once, and R3 already scores {{r2_r3_log}} because a big item stays big. Should the models be trained on percentages?

They already are. Every learned member predicts the four-month total as a ratio to the item's own recent level, in logs, and is trained on a quantile loss of that ratio with every item counted equally. Most inputs are normalised the same way: trend, momentum, seasonality and channel shares are ratios to the item's own level. Every version is worse than the plain average or level with it. The reason is that the best model for an item does not stay the best. None beats the plain average ({{mp_base}}). The gate cannot tell which model will be closest: it is right {{mp_acc_hi}} of the time from high-level features, and {{mp_acc_pos}} when it also sees where each model sits, against 20% by chance. Taking its first choice costs most ({{mp_gate_pick}}). Using its probabilities as weights is close to equal weights and still behind ({{mp_gate_soft}}). PCA shows why. The five models are mostly one shared view: the first component, with all five moving together, holds {{mp_pc1}} of the variation in their forecasts. The second ({{mp_pc2}}) is the rule agents against the four learned models. The other three hold {{mp_pc_rest}} between them. Regressing what sold on the components gives {{mp_pca_lo}} to {{mp_pca_hi}}, because a fitted model overfits the small components. The five have little independent information to separate. A better stack needs a member that knows something the five do not.

Three things handle it.

  1. Re-forecast after the first month. In {{al_m1}} of these forecasts the first month already ran well above pace. With the plans and one month of sales known, their error falls by {{al_cut_all}} and {{al_hit_all}} of them land within ±20%, against {{al_hit_ref}} without.
  2. The roll-out plan. This is the business's list of which stores each item will be sent to. {{al_st2}} of these forecasts are items that reached two or more additional own stores during the window, against {{al_st2_r}} of other forecasts. Their sales rose partly because they were on more shelves. The models could not know that on the forecast date; the business did. If the models are told what the business plans ahead (stores and partners added, discount depth, stock and sizes, new colours), the error on these forecasts is {{al_cut_plan}} smaller. The effect is modest because most of these misses are demand, not distribution.
  3. A watch list, for buying and for attention. The fifth of forecasts with the highest risk holds {{al_top_err}} of this error, and {{al_top_rate}} of that fifth turn out all-too-low. Raising the whole fifth by the factor seen at earlier origins ({{al_fac_lo}}–{{al_fac_hi}}) brings the overall bias from {{al_up_bias0}} to {{al_up_bias}} but leaves accuracy where it was ({{al_up_w}} against {{al_up_w0}} over the last {{al_up_n}} origins), because it also raises the flagged items that did not take off. So the flag should not move the forecast. It says which items to re-forecast first and where to buy above the forecast.

How the flag is identified. At each origin a classifier is trained on earlier forecasts whose results are known. Its target is yes or no: did all five models end below what sold. Its {{fi_n}} inputs are all known on the forecast date. It gives each new forecast a risk between 0 and 1, and the flag is the fifth of forecasts with the highest risk at that origin. {{fi_top}} of that fifth turn out all-too-low, against {{fi_low}} of the lowest fifth.

Entropy and past peaks as inputs. Asked in a comment on this page. Entropy measures how evenly an item's sales are spread: over the last 91 days, over its last 12 months and over channels, plus the share sold on its best seven days. Past peaks: months since the best month, the best month of the last year over the level, months above 1.5× the item's median, whether last year's peak month falls in the forecast window, and last year's same window over the level. Entropy tells how large the miss will be, not which way: evenness over 91 days correlates {{en_size}} with the size of the miss and {{en_dir}} with its direction, and the stated range already carries the size. In the correction: {{en_w_ent}} with entropy, {{en_w_peak}} with past peaks and {{en_w_both}} with both, against {{en_w_ref}} without. The flag stays at AUC {{en_auc}}. A peak block inside the tree model gave the same result earlier (D253).

Method

Definitions and limits

System. The submitted forecast (candidate_nx4, D350; the same units as candidate_nx3, with the D318 confidence rule and the D317 ranges): arithmetic mean of five members (rule agents, LightGBM, neural net, two TabPFN v2 variants) with the segment and breakout corrections (D273–D274), plus two hand rules on four of the 100 targets (out of stock, World Cup). The backtest scores the model system; the hand rules exist only in the 2026 workbook. Scores reproduce the log: {{ratio_recent}} recent, {{ratio_all}} on all 13.

Scores. WAPE ÷ R3 = Σ|forecast − actual| ÷ Σ|R3 − actual| on 4-month item totals. Absolute error = Σ|forecast − actual| ÷ Σ actual. Bias = Σ forecast ÷ Σ actual − 1; negative is under-forecast.

Hindsight signals use what happened in the forecast window. They show what information is missing; they are not forecasts. "Stores" counts own stores with at least one sale, so part of the store signal is demand itself. Stock gaps are inferred from improbable runs of zero-sale days (D021), not from inventory.

Not covered. Month-level errors inside the window, and the buying simulation. The season-memory test uses the tree model alone. Files: eda/198c–198g, data/fk_err_master.parquet, data/fk_err_analysis.json; decisions D276–D277 in log.md.
